I oppose this motion.

Strict laws to regulate LLMs would be a blunt, overreaching response to a fast-moving technology. The real danger is not that LLMs exist, but that heavy-handed regulation would freeze innovation, entrench large incumbents, and make the technology less accessible to the very people who could use it responsibly.

First, strict laws would punish legitimate use to stop speculative harm. LLMs are used for education, accessibility, translation, coding assistance, customer support, and research. If governments impose rigid legal barriers, startups, universities, and small developers will be the first to suffer. Large firms can absorb compliance costs; smaller ones cannot. So “safety” becomes a moat for the biggest players, not real protection for the public.

Second, strict laws are too slow for this technology. LLMs evolve in months, while legislation takes years. By the time a strict regulatory framework is written, the models, risks, and use cases will have changed. That means lawmakers will either write laws so broad they become harmful, or so specific they become obsolete. Flexible standards, industry best practices, and targeted enforcement against actual abuse are far more effective than sweeping legal control.

Third, the harms often come from misuse, not the model itself. Fraud, impersonation, and misinformation are already illegal. The answer is to enforce existing laws against bad actors, not create a restrictive regime for everyone else. We do not ban the printing press because some people print propaganda; we punish the abuse. The same logic should apply to LLMs.

Finally, strict regulation can reduce transparency, not increase it. If rules become too burdensome, companies may centralize development and hide information to avoid liability. Open research and competition are better safeguards because they allow scrutiny, testing, and rapid improvement. A highly regulated environment may feel responsible, but in practice it often drives development underground or into a few unaccountable hands.

The right approach is not strict laws. It is targeted, proportionate oversight that punishes actual harm without strangling innovation. We should regulate outcomes, not suffocate the tools themselves.

Strict laws on LLMs would do more damage than good. For that reason, I strongly oppose the motion.
